\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The primary pillar-UDP paper proves the protocol's \emph{qualitative} delivery
guarantees---exactly-once processing, route-around liveness, and bounded
fan-out---and defers the \emph{quantitative} design to this companion. We supply
three things. First, the analytic loss/bandwidth trade that sets the
per-connection \emph{redundancy allowance}: a closed form for the minimum copy
count $\Wstar=\lceil\log_p\varepsilon\rceil$ in the small-message regime and a
binomial residual for the erasure-coded bulk regime, with the overhead each
implies. Second, a correlated-loss model---an exchangeable P\'olya/Beta-binomial
mixture with a single correlation parameter $\rho$---that quantifies exactly how
much shared loss erodes redundancy, showing that the whole benefit of pillar-UDP
rides on driving $\rho$ toward zero by AS-diverse dispersion, and turning the
``paths are independent'' claim from an assumption into a measurable quantity
with a stated methodology. Third, the concrete header wire format and an adaptive
control loop that sets the allowance from measured loss while remaining
\emph{congestion-safe}: a delay-gradient discriminator prevents redundancy from
being added in response to congestion (as opposed to link loss), and a
marginal-gain rule plus an aggregate-egress bound cap fan-out so the adaptive
allowance cannot itself induce collapse. Every numeric result here is exact
arithmetic from the closed forms; the one empirical question---the real-world
distribution of $\rho$---is framed as a measurement study, not filled with
invented data.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
pillar-UDP delivers a message by spraying a \emph{bounded} number of redundant,
content-addressed datagrams across a topologically dispersed set of nodes and
letting the receiver deduplicate by CID; loss is absorbed by redundancy and
congestion is avoided by finding a good route rather than by throttling a
window. The primary paper~\cite{primary} model-checks that this delivers exactly
once and always routes around lossy links whenever the cell stays reachable by
some dispersed path. What it deliberately leaves open is every \emph{number}: how
many copies, how much bandwidth that costs, how to encode the choice on the wire,
how to adapt it to a changing link, and how to keep the adaptation from becoming
a self-inflicted congestion source. This paper closes those gaps.

The engineering tension is a single trade. Redundancy buys reliability
super-linearly ($p^{W}$ falls fast in $W$) but costs bandwidth linearly (fan-out
$W$). Setting the redundancy allowance is therefore an optimisation with three
inputs---the measured per-path loss $p$, the connection's target residual
$\varepsilon$, and the correlation $\rho$ between the paths the copies take---and
one hard constraint: the aggregate traffic a cell emits must never exceed what
its egress can carry, or the ``cure'' becomes the disease. We treat each in turn.

\section{System model}
A \emph{message} is a small, signed, content-addressed unit identified by its
CID. A \emph{connection} carries a stream of messages between a client and a cell
or between two cells and declares a target residual loss $\varepsilon$ (the
probability a message is ultimately undelivered that the application will
tolerate). A \emph{path} is one dispersed source/route a copy takes; the sender
controls how many paths it uses. We write $p$ for the per-path loss probability
over the averaging window and treat per-path losses as a marginal-$p$ Bernoulli
whose \emph{dependence} across paths is captured by a correlation parameter
$\rho\in[0,1]$ (\S\ref{sec:corr}); $\rho=0$ is fully independent, $\rho=1$ is one
shared fate. Two redundancy shapes share this substrate: \emph{copy redundancy}
(a whole message sent $W$ times, for small messages) and \emph{erasure
redundancy} ($K$ data $+$ $M$ parity shards, reconstructable from any $K$, for
bulk). Both are bounded by the same per-connection allowance field
(\S\ref{sec:header}).

\section{The loss/bandwidth trade: small messages}\label{sec:trade}
Under independent per-path loss, a message sent as $W$ copies is undelivered only
if all $W$ drop:
\begin{equation}
  P_{\text{fail}}(W,p) = p^{\,W}. \label{eq:pw}
\end{equation}
The bandwidth cost is a fan-out of $W$ (overhead $W-1$ redundant copies per
delivered message). Fixing the connection's target $\varepsilon$ and solving
$p^{W}\le\varepsilon$ gives the minimum allowance in closed form:
\begin{equation}
  \Wstar(p,\varepsilon)=\left\lceil\frac{\ln\varepsilon}{\ln p}\right\rceil
   =\left\lceil\log_{p}\varepsilon\right\rceil. \label{eq:wstar}
\end{equation}
This is the value the sender computes each averaging window and writes into the
allowance field. It scales \emph{down} to $W=1$ as $p\to0$ (a clean path costs
nothing beyond the message itself) and \emph{up} as $p\to1$, which is precisely
the adaptive posture the primary paper requires and the opposite of a fixed
redundancy multiplier. Table~\ref{tab:wstar} tabulates $\Wstar$; it is exact
arithmetic from~\eqref{eq:wstar}. Even a $30\%$-loss path needs only
$W{=}12$ copies for a one-in-a-million residual, and a $10\%$ path needs $6$.

\paragraph{Efficiency.}
Delivered-message yield per unit bandwidth is $(1-p^{W})/W$: it is maximised at
$W{=}1$ and decreases monotonically, so redundancy is never ``free
throughput''---it is reliability purchased with bandwidth. The correct operating
point is therefore not an efficiency optimum but the \emph{smallest} $W$ meeting
$\varepsilon$, i.e.\ $\Wstar$; spending more wastes bandwidth and spending less
misses the target. This is why the allowance is a hard per-connection budget and
why the controller of \S\ref{sec:control} tracks $\Wstar$ rather than hunting a
throughput maximum.

\paragraph{Latency.}
Because the $W$ copies travel in parallel rather than as RTT-spaced
retransmissions, delivery latency is the \emph{minimum} arrival time over the
surviving copies, not a retransmit timeout after a loss. Redundancy thus improves
tail latency on lossy paths at the same time as it improves delivery---the
property single-path protocols cannot get, since their only recovery is to wait
an RTT and resend.

\section{Correlated loss and the independence assumption}\label{sec:corr}
Equation~\eqref{eq:pw} assumes the $W$ paths fail independently. That assumption
is the load-bearing one: if the copies share a bottleneck, redundancy buys far
less. We make the dependence explicit with an exchangeable mixture. Let the
common ``network weather'' over the copies be a latent loss level $q$ with mean
$\mathbb{E}[q]=p$ drawn from a Beta$(a,b)$ distribution; conditioned on $q$ the
$W$ copies drop independently. Then
\begin{equation}
  P_{\text{fail}}(W,p,\rho)=\mathbb{E}[q^{W}]
   =\prod_{i=0}^{W-1}\frac{p\varphi+i}{\varphi+i},\qquad
   \varphi=\frac{1-\rho}{\rho}, \label{eq:corr}
\end{equation}
where $\rho=1/(a{+}b{+}1)$ is the pairwise loss correlation and $\varphi=a{+}b$.
This is a P\'olya/Beta-binomial urn and it interpolates exactly between the two
extremes: as $\rho\to0$ ($\varphi\to\infty$), \eqref{eq:corr} $\to p^{W}$, the
independent case; as $\rho\to1$ ($\varphi\to0$), it $\to p$---i.e.\ $W$ copies on
one shared fate are worth a single copy. By Jensen's inequality
$\mathbb{E}[q^{W}]\ge(\mathbb{E}q)^{W}=p^{W}$, so \emph{any} positive correlation
strictly erodes redundancy, and the erosion compounds with $W$.

Figure~\ref{fig:corr} plots \eqref{eq:corr} for $p{=}0.2$. The message is stark:
at $\rho{=}0$ ten copies reach $10^{-7}$; at a modest $\rho{=}0.2$ the same ten
copies stall near $10^{-2.7}$ and adding more barely helps; at $\rho{=}0.5$
redundancy is nearly worthless. \emph{This is the analytic justification for
dispersion.} pillar-ipam binds addresses to topology tiers and sites hold
distinct prefixes, so ``spray from a dispersed set of IPs'' is exactly the
operation that pushes the copies onto uncorrelated failure domains and drives
$\rho\to0$. Dispersion is not a nicety; Eq.~\eqref{eq:corr} shows it is the
difference between redundancy that works and redundancy that does not.

\paragraph{Making $\rho$ measurable (methodology, not results).}
Equation~\eqref{eq:corr} reduces the open empirical question to a single
estimand: the pairwise loss correlation $\rho$ between dispersed pillar paths,
and its dependence on how much topology diversity separates them. We do
\emph{not} report a value here---measuring it requires a live multi-site
deployment we treat as future work---but we fix the method so the number, once
gathered, plugs straight into the model. (i)~From concurrent per-path
delivery logs across pairs of pillar-ipam prefixes at increasing topology
distance (same rack, same site, same region, cross-region), estimate the
empirical joint drop rate and invert \eqref{eq:corr} for $\rho$ at each distance.
(ii)~Fit the burst structure with a two-state Gilbert--Elliott
channel~\cite{gilbert,elliott} per path and measure cross-path state
correlation, which yields $\rho$ directly. (iii)~Feed the measured
$\rho(\text{distance})$ back into $\Wstar$ by replacing $p$ in \eqref{eq:wstar}
with the correlation-inflated effective loss from \eqref{eq:corr}. Until those
measurements exist, the controller (\S\ref{sec:control}) uses a conservative
$\rho$ prior and the safety envelope (\S\ref{sec:safety}) does not depend on the
value.

\section{Bulk transfer: erasure coding}\label{sec:erasure}
For payloads larger than a datagram, copy redundancy is wasteful: sending a
megabyte $W$ times to survive loss pays an integer multiple. Bulk instead uses a
systematic erasure code---$K$ data shards plus $M$ parity shards, $N=K+M$
total, any $K$ of which reconstruct the payload (the implementation's
\code{encode}/\code{reconstruct} in \code{pillar\_udp.rs}). Under independent
per-shard loss $p$, reconstruction fails iff fewer than $K$ shards arrive:
\begin{equation}
  P_{\text{fail}}(K,M,p)=\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\binom{N}{i}(1-p)^{i}p^{\,N-i}.
  \label{eq:erasure}
\end{equation}
The overhead is $M/K$, a \emph{fractional} rate tunable independently of $K$,
where copy redundancy pays a whole multiple. Figure~\ref{fig:erasure} plots
\eqref{eq:erasure}: at $20\%$ loss, $K{=}8,M{=}8$ (overhead $1.0$) reaches
$10^{-2.8}$, and increasing the block to $K{=}16,M{=}8$ (overhead $0.5$) does
about as well because a larger block averages the loss. The minimum parity
$M^\star(K,p,\varepsilon)$ meeting a target follows by the same solve as
$\Wstar$; Table~\ref{tab:mstar} reports it for $K{=}8,\varepsilon{=}10^{-6}$. The
mapping is the design rule: the single allowance field encodes a parity
\emph{rate} (\S\ref{sec:header}) and the sender derives $M=\lceil\text{rate}\cdot
K\rceil$ from it and the block size $K$.

\section{Adaptive allowance control}\label{sec:control}
The allowance is not static: the sender measures loss and re-derives it. Let
$\hat p_t$ be an exponentially weighted estimate of per-path loss updated per
acknowledgement/CID-arrival observation $\ell_t\in\{0,1\}$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat p_t=(1-\alpha)\hat p_{t-1}+\alpha\,\ell_t,\qquad 0<\alpha\ll1,
\end{equation}
and set $W_t=\mathrm{clamp}\big(\Wstar(\hat p_t,\varepsilon),\,1,\,W_{\max}\big)$
(or the parity analogue for bulk). Three properties make this stable where a
window-based controller is not:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Monotone, memoryless map.} $\Wstar(\cdot,\varepsilon)$ is a fixed
  monotone step function of $\hat p_t$; the only state is the EWMA, a contraction
  of bounded inputs, so $W_t$ cannot diverge or oscillate absent boundary
  chatter.
  \item \textbf{Hysteresis at the steps.} $\Wstar$ jumps at discrete $\hat p$
  thresholds; we only change $W$ when $\hat p_t$ crosses a threshold by a margin
  $\delta$, eliminating flapping when $\hat p$ sits on a boundary.
  \item \textbf{No positive feedback.} The control target is \emph{loss}, and---
  crucially---redundancy is added only for \emph{link} loss, not congestion
  (\S\ref{sec:safety}). On dispersed independent paths, raising $W$ does not raise
  the $\hat p$ that drove it, so the loop has no self-reinforcing term. This is
  the structural difference from TCP/QUIC, whose control variable (the window)
  directly drives the congestion signal it reacts to.
\end{enumerate}
$W_{\max}$ is the connection's declared allowance ceiling; the controller may
request up to it but never beyond, so a misbehaving loss estimate can never
unbound fan-out.

\section{Congestion safety}\label{sec:safety}
Redundancy is dangerous in exactly one regime: when the loss it is reacting to is
\emph{congestion} rather than link degradation. Adding copies to a congested
bottleneck worsens the congestion---the classic collapse. pillar-UDP stays safe
by three composable mechanisms.

\paragraph{1. Discriminate link loss from congestion.}
Congestion raises queueing delay \emph{and} loss together; link loss raises loss
with roughly flat one-way delay. The sender tracks a one-way delay gradient (the
signal LEDBAT~\cite{ledbat} and delay-based controllers use) and attributes to
the link-loss component only the loss that is \emph{not} accompanied by a rising
delay gradient. Redundancy scales with the link-loss component; when the delay
gradient indicates congestion, the controller \emph{reduces} offered rate (and
$W$) exactly like a well-behaved flow, rather than adding copies.

\paragraph{2. Marginal-gain stop.}
Even absent an explicit $\varepsilon$, the marginal delivery gain of the $W$-th
copy is $\partial_W(1-p^{W})=p^{W}\ln(1/p)$, which falls geometrically: for
$p{=}0.3$ it is $0.36,0.11,0.033,0.0098,\dots$ for $W{=}1,2,3,4$. The controller
stops increasing $W$ once the marginal delivered-probability per extra copy falls
below a threshold $\tau$, giving a natural cap that no target can push past on a
good path.

\paragraph{3. Aggregate egress bound.}
Redundancy is admitted against capacity. For a node with egress capacity $C$
carrying flows $f$ at rates $\lambda_f$ with mean message size $S_f$ and
allowance $W_f$, the local admission invariant is
\begin{equation}
  \sum_f \lambda_f\,W_f\,S_f \;\le\; C. \label{eq:admit}
\end{equation}
Each node enforces \eqref{eq:admit} on its own egress; because redundant copies
are \emph{dispersed} across nodes and paths, any single bottleneck link carries
only a fraction of a flow's fan-out, so the bound binds on aggregate egress, not
on any one path. Together these ensure the adaptive allowance can raise
reliability without ever becoming a congestion source: it responds to the loss it
can fix (the link) and stands down on the loss it would worsen (congestion).

\section{Header wire format}\label{sec:header}
Every datagram---spray copy, forward, reply, or acknowledgement---carries the
header below, which binds it to its logical message and states the connection's
redundancy contract up front. Fields are fixed-width and big-endian; optional
fields are gated by flag bits so the common small-message header stays compact.

\paragraph{Framing and MTU.}
On Route~A the header and payload ride as the application framing inside the
Noise-encrypted, yamux-muxed stream; on Route~B they are the native transport
frame. Datagrams are sized to the conservative IPv6 minimum path MTU: a
small-message datagram keeps header~$+$~payload~$\le 1200$~bytes, and each bulk
shard is sized so header~$+$~shard~$\le 1200$, so neither fragments. The header is
therefore a fixed $35$--$59$-byte prefix; for a $1$~KB message its overhead is
$3.4\%$, dwarfed by the $\times W$ payload cost, so the redundancy allowance, not
the header, dominates on-wire bytes---as intended.

\paragraph{Worked examples.}
A small idempotent spray copy with $W{=}4$, $\text{TTL}{=}6$, no shard, no token:
\begin{center}\code{20 <32-byte CID> 04 06 <payload>}\end{center}
(\code{0x20}$=$ version~1, type spray, convergent). A bulk shard from a $K{=}8,
M{=}4$ block, index~3, of a $1{,}048{,}576$-byte payload:
\begin{center}\code{22 <CID> 90 06 08 04 0003 00100000 <shard>}\end{center}
(\code{0x22} sets has-shard; \code{0x90} is parity mode at rate $16/32{=}0.5$;
the descriptor carries $K{=}8,M{=}4,\text{idx}{=}3,\text{len}{=}0\text{x}100000$).

\section{Relation to the verified models}
Every header field corresponds to an invariant already machine-checked in the
primary paper's TLA\textsuperscript{+} specifications, so the wire format is an
encoding of proven mechanics, not new untested behaviour: the CID is the dedup
key behind \code{ExactlyOnce}; the allowance bounds fan-out behind
\code{SprayWidthBounded}/\code{BoundedTotalDatagrams}; the hop TTL is the
forwarding-termination budget behind \code{NoForwardingLoops}; the effect-class
bit is the idempotent/exclusive split routing exclusive processing to the
coordination-core lease holder; and the RR token is the return-routability proof
behind \code{AntiAmplificationBound}~\cite{primary}. This paper adds the
\emph{quantities}---$\Wstar$, $M^\star$, the $\rho$ model, the control loop, and
the safety envelope---that the qualitative proofs deliberately abstract away.

\section{Related work}
The loss-cliff that motivates the protocol is documented by Head~\cite{grayson};
the idea of surviving loss with redundant, source-dispersed copies rather than
retransmission descends from real-time game networking~\cite{tribes}. Bursty loss
is classically modelled by the Gilbert--Elliott two-state
channel~\cite{gilbert,elliott}, which we use to motivate the correlation
estimand. Erasure coding for reliable transport spans Reed--Solomon
codes~\cite{reedsolomon}, fountain codes such as RaptorQ~\cite{raptorq}, and
on-the-fly coding such as Tetrys~\cite{tetrys}; pillar-UDP's bulk regime is a
systematic block code selected by \eqref{eq:erasure}. QUIC~\cite{quic} and its
loss/congestion control~\cite{quicrecovery} exemplify the single-path,
window-throttled posture we contrast against; LEDBAT~\cite{ledbat} supplies the
delay-gradient discriminator we borrow for congestion safety; and
MPTCP~\cite{mptcp} shares the multipath aim but retains per-subflow window
control rather than redundancy. The correctness scaffolding is
TLA\textsuperscript{+}~\cite{tlaplus}.

\section{Limitations and future work}
The analysis is exact but rests on two modelling choices whose empirical
calibration is future work: the marginal-$p$ Bernoulli-with-correlation loss
model (\S\ref{sec:corr}), whose single parameter $\rho$ must be measured across
real dispersed pillar paths by the methodology given rather than assumed; and the
independence \emph{within} a block for erasure (\S\ref{sec:erasure}), which the
same correlation study should validate for shards as it does for copies. The
delay-gradient discriminator (\S\ref{sec:safety}) inherits the known difficulty
of delay-based congestion signals under reordering and needs the same live
validation. None of these affects the proven safety and liveness guarantees,
which hold for any loss pattern; they affect only how tightly the allowance can
be tuned. We report no measured numbers here precisely because we have not run
that study; the framework is built so its results drop in without changing any
equation.

\section{Conclusion}
Setting pillar-UDP's redundancy is a bounded optimisation: choose the smallest
allowance---copy count $\Wstar$ or parity $M^\star$---that meets the connection's
residual target at the measured loss, adapt it as loss changes, and never let the
aggregate exceed egress. The reliability it buys is real and super-linear, but
only to the extent the dispersed paths are uncorrelated: Eq.~\eqref{eq:corr}
makes that dependence exact and turns ``spray from diverse IPs'' from folklore
into the quantitative lever it is. With the wire format fixed and the control
loop kept congestion-safe by construction, the allowance becomes a single,
well-understood knob spanning a clean link (where $W{=}1$ costs nothing) to a
hostile one (where redundancy delivers what window backoff cannot).
